# Prior-art search: has anyone already linked Biblical Hebrew *nefesh* to self-preservation, *conatus*, or homeostasis?

## Your role

You are a research librarian and a Hebrew Bible scholar, with working knowledge of Semitic philology, cognitive linguistics, and the philosophy of emotion. Your job in this session is **not** to evaluate whether the idea below is correct. Your job is to find out whether it — or something close to it — **has already been proposed in scholarly or serious intellectual literature**, and to report that honestly.

## Why this matters

The author of the idea is an independent researcher who is considering presenting it to academic linguists and Hebrew Bible scholars. Before doing so, the author needs to know whether the idea is new, partly anticipated, or already published. An inflated "nobody has said this" is far more harmful to the author than an honest "X said something close in 1987". **Finding prior art is a success, not a failure.** Missing it would embarrass the author in front of the very scholars they want to approach.

## The idea being checked

> **Nefesh is the lived experience of a living creature's striving to persist in its life (the drive to self-preservation).**
> It is not an entity the creature *has*; it is the creature itself, viewed as one who needs, desires, is threatened, and recovers. The throat, mouth and breath are where this striving is felt; blood is the substance it depends on.

The idea claims that this single schema motivates the whole semantic range of *nefesh*:

1. **Breath**: it goes in, stops, and leaves at death (Gen 35:18; Ps 69:2).
2. **Appetite**: the drive to swallow, eat and drink, extended metaphorically (Prov 27:7; Isa 5:14, Sheol's *nefesh*).
3. **Restoration**: the Niphal verb *wayyinnāpēš*, "catch one's breath / recover", in Exod 23:12, Exod 31:17 (said of God) and 2 Sam 16:14. This is read as a return to equilibrium, i.e. homeostasis.
4. **Attraction and aversion**: disgust and loathing verbs (*gāʿal*, *qûṣ*, *qāṣar*). This is the *only* field applied to God's *nefesh*, and is read as a protective response.
5. **Life itself**: sought, rescued, ransomed; *nefesh* is named exactly when life is at stake (Gen 19:17; Exod 21:23).
6. **Livelihood**: Deut 24:6, the millstone "is *nefesh*". Compare Akkadian *napištu*, "livelihood, provisions".
7. **Blood**: shedding it harms the striving (Lev 17:11; Deut 12:23).
8. **The creature as bearer of the striving**: *nefesh ḥayyâ* defined through food (Gen 1:30). Man *becomes* a *nefesh* (Gen 2:7).
9. **Lives in someone's hand**: counted *nefashot* are dependents or captives whose lives hang on a holder, who either sustains them (responsibility, feeding: Lev 22:11; Exod 16:16) or takes them (captivity, sale, *ḥerem*). Compare Job 12:10. A related claim: military censuses count "skulls/heads" (Num 1:2), while the ransom is for the *nefesh* (Exod 30:12).
10. **Extension of self to another**: love as including the other in what one preserves (1 Sam 18:3, "loved him as his own *nefesh*"). Compare Esarhaddon's Succession Treaty §24, *kī napšātekunu*.
11. **Reflexive "self"**: used mostly when the self is endangered, needy, or being regulated. The claim is that the reflexive use is *marked*, not bleached.
12. **The dead**: the striving continues after death, but now the living must supply it. Katumuwa stele, a ram "for my *nbš* that is in this stele"; *nefesh* = funerary monument; *nefesh mēt*.

The explicit intellectual anchors the author uses are:
- **Spinoza**, *Ethics* III P6–P7 (*conatus*: each thing strives to persevere in its being, and this striving is its actual essence).
- **Antonio Damasio**: feeling as the mental expression of homeostasis.
- **Rozin, Haidt & McCauley**: disgust originating in oral food rejection.

**Closest known positions** (these are background, *not* what you are looking for):
- **Dürr 1925**: *nefesh* = throat.
- **H. W. Wolff 1973**: "*nepeš* – Needy Man", i.e. neediness and lack.
- **Pedersen 1926 / H. W. Robinson 1925**: the totality of the person.
- **Steiner 2015 / Pleijel 2019 / Schaper 2020 / Suriano 2018 / Sanders 2013**: separable or post-mortem *nefesh*, and the "appetites of the dead".

The question is whether anyone has gone **beyond** these, to the specific claim that *nefesh* = the striving or drive to self-preservation, *conatus*, or homeostatic self-maintenance, as the unifying principle.

For fuller context you may read Part B ("חלק ב׳") of `ניתוחים-לשוניים-בינה-מלאכותית/נפש.ai.inline.rtl.md` in this repository. Read it only for context. Do not let it shape your judgment of what counts as prior art.

## What counts as prior art — classify every find into one of these tiers

- **Tier A — Anticipation.** Someone explicitly proposes that *nefesh* (or a Semitic cognate: *napištu*, *npš*, *nbš*, *nafs*) is fundamentally the drive or striving for self-preservation, *conatus*, self-maintenance, or homeostasis, as a unifying or core meaning.
- **Tier B — Partial anticipation.** Someone links *nefesh* to self-preservation, *conatus* or homeostasis in passing or for a subset of uses, or states one of the specific sub-claims above in a way that overlaps substantially. Examples: God's *nefesh* as aversion/self-protection; counted *nefashot* as lives dependent on a holder; the reflexive use as marked; the Niphal verb as homeostatic recovery.
- **Tier C — Adjacent.** Work that uses the same conceptual tools on neighbouring material. Examples: embodiment or cognitive-linguistic analyses of *nefesh*; *conatus* or homeostasis applied to biblical anthropology in general; Arabic *nafs* and self-preservation (for example the Islamic legal category *ḥifẓ al-nafs*); Jewish-philosophical readings of *nefesh* as vital striving. Also relevant: any connection between Spinoza's *conatus* and Hebrew. Spinoza wrote a Hebrew grammar, the *Compendium grammatices linguae hebraeae*. Check specifically whether he, or anyone writing about him, connects *conatus* to *nefesh*.
- **Not prior art.** General statements that *nefesh* means "life" or "vitality", or Wolff-style "neediness", *without* the striving/self-preservation framing. Mention only if they are the closest thing you found.

## Search strategy

Search broadly and persistently. A single query that returns nothing proves nothing. Vary terms, languages and databases before concluding. You may run independent search threads in parallel (for example with subagents: one per language or database cluster).

**Query families.** Combine them and vary the spelling: nefesh / nephesh / nepeš / næpæš / nefeš.
- nefesh + "self-preservation" / "preservation of life" / "will to live" / "drive to live" / "vital drive" / "striving"
- nefesh + conatus / Spinoza / "persevere in its being"
- nefesh + homeostasis / Damasio / "life regulation" / "self-maintenance"
- nefesh + embodiment / "embodied cognition" / "cognitive linguistics" / "conceptual metaphor" / "image schema" / "prototype"
- nefesh + disgust / "God's nefesh" / anthropopathism
- *wayyinnāpēš* / "Exodus 31:17" + breath / "catch his breath" / refresh + nefesh
- German: Nefesch / näfäš + Selbsterhaltung / Lebenswille / Lebenstrieb / conatus / Homöostase / Selbstbehauptung
- French: nèphèsh / nefesh + "conservation de soi" / "instinct de conservation" / conatus
- Hebrew: נפש + "שימור עצמי" / "הישרדות" / "יצר החיים" / קונטוס / "שפינוזה" / הומאוסטזיס
- Arabic/Islamic adjacent: nafs + "self-preservation" / "ḥifẓ al-nafs" + etymology / Semitic

**Where to search:**
- Google Scholar, Google Books (snippet search inside books), JSTOR, Brill, De Gruyter, SAGE, SBL Press (open-access titles), Academia.edu, ResearchGate, PhilPapers.
- Theology and Bible indexes reachable online: IxTheo, RAMBI (National Library of Israel), BiBIL.
- Dissertations: ProQuest open listings, university repositories.
- Hebrew publications (for example Beit Mikra, Shnaton, Leshonenu).

**People and works to check specifically.** Search their work *for the striving/self-preservation framing*:
- **Philologists and anthropologists of the OT:** H. W. Wolff; C. Westermann; H. Seebass; B. Janowski and the Tübingen "constellative anthropology" circle; A. Wagner (*Emotionen, Gefühle und Sprache im Alten Testament*, and his work on body terms); R. Steiner; R. Pleijel; J. Schaper; M. Suriano; S. Sanders; M. McAffee (*Life and Mortality in Ugaritic*).
- **Cognitive-linguistic and emotion scholarship on Biblical Hebrew:** e.g. E. van Wolde; Z. Kotzé; P. Kruger; F. Mirguet; and others you identify.
- **Philosophers:** anyone who connects Spinoza's *conatus* to biblical Hebrew anthropology. Includes Jewish philosophers and Spinoza scholars writing on his Hebrew grammar.
- **Scientists and popularisers:** anyone who connects Damasio's homeostasis framework to *nefesh* or *nafs*. Damasio himself mentions Hebrew or biblical ideas in places — check.

**Stopping rule.** Stop searching a thread when additional varied queries keep returning only material you have already classified. Report roughly how many distinct queries you ran per language.

## Verification rules — strict

- **An item you cannot find online is treated as a hallucination and must not be reported as a finding.** Do not cite from memory. Every cited work must have a working URL that you actually opened or saw in results. Acceptable URLs: a publisher page, DOI, JSTOR, Google Books, a repository or catalog.
- **For every Tier A or B item, quote the exact relevant sentence(s)** from the source (original language, plus an English translation if needed), with a page number when available.
  - If you only saw a snippet or an abstract, say so explicitly: "seen: abstract only" / "seen: Google Books snippet" / "seen: full text".
  - If you infer the content without seeing the wording, label it "inferred — not verified" and do **not** rank it above Tier C.
- **Confirm identity.** Check that the author, year, title and venue belong to the same work, and that it is not a similarly named work.
- **Do not upgrade an item's tier** because it would make the report more interesting. When in doubt, choose the lower tier and explain why.

## Output

Write your findings to **`misc/פנייה לבלשנים/existing-articles-search - results.md`**. Use this structure:

1. **תקציר בעברית (Hebrew summary, 5–10 lines).** The bottom line: is the idea new, partly anticipated, or anticipated? Name the closest one to three works.
2. **Verdict (English).** One of:
   - *No anticipation found*
   - *Partial anticipation*
   - *Substantially anticipated*

   Add a one-paragraph justification and a confidence level (low / medium / high), with the main reason for the uncertainty.
3. **Findings table, ordered by tier (A, then B, then C).** Columns:

   | Tier | Author, year, title, venue | URL | What they claim (exact quote + page) | How it overlaps with / differs from the idea | Seen as (full text / snippet / abstract) |

4. **Sub-claim map.** For each of the 12 components listed above, state one of:
   - "anticipated by X"
   - "partially by X"
   - "no prior statement found"

   Many sub-claims may turn out to be novel even if the overall framing is not, or the reverse. This map is the most useful part for the author.
5. **What the idea would need to cite.** The minimal list of works a reviewer would expect the author to engage with.
6. **Search log.** Languages, databases, number of distinct queries per language, and notable queries that returned nothing.
7. **Items considered and rejected.** Works that looked relevant but were not, with one line on why. Include anything you could not verify online, clearly marked as excluded.

Write the file in English, except the Hebrew summary. Keep quotations in their original language. Be concise: aim for clarity over length. Do not reproduce the author's research file, and do not argue for or against the idea itself beyond what is needed to explain overlap.
