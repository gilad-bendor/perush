## Key findings (Round 5 run 2, 2026-10-31)
**Gen 1:21 – the serpent of the waters (IL, HE).** Two Anatolian serpent stories, neither of them a creation story. In
Illuyanka the Storm-god is beaten first and wins only by a trick – a feast, a mortal helper (Ḫupašiya) who demands the
goddess's bed, the serpent too drunk to return to its hole and bound with a cord (↔ Job 40:25, 29); in the second version
the serpent takes the god's heart and eyes. In Ḫedammu the voracious sea serpent, son of Kumarbi and the Sea's daughter,
is undone by Ištar bathing naked before him (*nekumant-* `עירום`, HE 4:23, 8:20 ↔ Gen 2:25 – the only naked scene of the
corpus; the brief's naked Ištar in Ullikummi is not on the tablet: she dresses and adorns herself, UL 2:82). Ea's speech
(HE 1:52–61): destroying mankind would leave the gods without offerings – the Storm-god would have to plough and Ištar
and Ḫebat grind at the millstone themselves (↔ Gen 8:20–22 and Atra-ḫasīs III iii 30–31, v 34–35). The god of Gen 1:21
creates the `תַּנִּינִם` and is never beaten: in both Hittite myths the order of the world is in danger and must be won back.

**Gen 6:1–4, 9:20–27 – the generations of the gods (KU).** Kingship in heaven passes by revolt: Alalu, Anu, Kumarbi,
each serving his predecessor nine years before deposing him. Kumarbi bites Anu's **loins** (*paršina-*, KU 35: not the
"knees" of the older literature, a rejected emendation) and swallows his manhood, and becomes pregnant with the Storm-god,
the Tigris and Tašmišu (↔ Hesiod's Ouranos and Kronos, translated in `../הסיודוס/`; ↔ Gen 9:22 [divergence], and as
reception only b. Sanh. 70a, Ham castrating Noah). Kumarbi is given a stone to eat (KU 143 ↔ the stone Kronos swallows),
the stone becomes a cult stone (KU 157 ↔ Gen 28:22), a god is born from his head (KU 117–118 ↔ Athena). Gen 6:1–4 has the
sons of the gods and the `גִּבֹּרִים`, but no succession in heaven at all.

**Gen 11:1–9 and Gen 1:6–8 – the stone that reaches heaven (UL; SR's tower).** Ullikummi, a basalt child set on the
shoulder of Upelluri, the giant who carries heaven and earth, grows until he reaches heaven and Kumme (UL 1:204, 3.1:30 ↔
Gen 11:4 [divergence]: a monster, not a city; no "head" in the Hittite, so `ראשו בשמים` was never written). Ea cuts him
off with "the old copper cutter with which they cut heaven and earth apart" (UL 3.1:219, 234: *kuer-* `חתך`, the tool
`מַחְתֵּךְ`) – the separation of heaven and earth as a past event of craft, against Gen 1:6–7's separation by the word
(no `הִבְדִּיל`, no `רָקִיעַ` in any line). Upelluri did not even notice (↔ Atlas). The Song of Release's tower parable
(AN.ZA.GÀR: bricks, foundations down to the netherworld, its top brought near heaven) is the corpus's closest match to Gen
11:3–4 in words.

**Lev 25:10, Deut 15, Jer 34 – the Song of Release (SR).** The Storm-god demands that Ebla release the captives of
Ikinkališ; Megi pleads, the assembly (Zazalla) refuses, the god threatens to break the city like a vessel (↔ Jer 34:8–22, a
release proclaimed and revoked and the city made "without inhabitant", the strongest parallel of the run; Jer 19:11).
*parā tarnumar* is rendered `שחרור` and the verb `שִׁלַּח` (↔ Deut 15:13, 18); `דְּרוֹר` is never in a line – the link to
Lev 25:10 runs through the Hurrian *kirenzi* = Akk. *andurāru*, the cognate of `דְּרוֹר`, not through the Hittite word.
The parables ("it is not a deer, it is a man who…") ↔ Judg 9:7–15, 2 Sam 12:1–7, Isa 29:16 / 45:9.

**Exod 2:3 – Zalpa (ZA).** The queen of Kaneš bears thirty sons in one year, fills containers with fat and sets them in
the river, which carries them to the sea at Zalpa (↔ Exod 2:3 and the Sargon legend, [divergence]: thirty, exposed, not
one, saved); the gods raise them; the sons, unknowing, are about to marry their thirty sisters (↔ Lev 18:9, Gen 19:30–38);
the youngest's objection is broken on the tablet (no negation preserved: `[…] מוּתָּר`).

**Gen 15, 18, 25 – Appu (AP).** The rich childless man of Šudul who lies down in his shoes; the Sun-god's promise "the
gods will give you one son" (AP 1:59; ↔ Gen 15:2–4, 18:10); two sons named Wrong and Right (`רַע`, `יָשָׁר`), the
inheritance divided, the Sun-god cursed by Wrong and the case passed to Ištar (↔ Gen 13:8–12, 25:22–27).

**Gen 14:19, 22 and Gen 39 – Elkunirša (EL).** The name is *ʾil qōnê ʾarṣ*, "El, creator (owner) of the earth"
[cognate] ↔ `אֵל עֶלְיוֹן קֹנֵה שָׁמַיִם וָאָרֶץ` (Gen 14:19); his wife Ašertu (= Athirat / Asherah) asks the Storm-god
(Baal) "sleep with me", is refused and slanders him to her husband (↔ Gen 39:7–20 [equivalent]); Ištar overhears as a cup
in El's hand and as a bird on the wall.

**Ps 44:24, Jer 14, 1 Kgs 18:27 – Telipinu (TE).** The god who goes away in anger and the world that stops: the mist
seizes the windows, the logs choke on the hearth, the ewe and the cow reject their young (↔ Jer 14:5, the hind that
abandons her newborn), grain and conception cease; the bee wakes the sleeping god (↔ Ps 44:24, 78:65, 1 Kgs 18:27), and
the anger is buried in bronze cauldrons with lead lids in the dark earth: "what goes in does not come out". The return
undoes the disaster word for word.

## For the ancient mind
A pantheon in dynastic politics (kingship in heaven won by revolt, viziers, messengers with staffs and wind-shoes); gods
who depend on human offerings and know it; monsters made on purpose by a deposed god; heaven and earth once cut apart with
a tool; festivals that re-enact the myth (Nerik's *purulli*, told by the priest Kella); a mortal who bargains with a
goddess and loses his family; the analogical magic of Kamrušepa ("as this wax melts…"); a city tale that turns into
politics (Zalpa against Ḫattuša); the humour of Appu; a song about debt-slavery and the god who demands release.
