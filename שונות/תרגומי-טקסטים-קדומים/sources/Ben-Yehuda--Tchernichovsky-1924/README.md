# Shaul Tchernichovsky, *עלילות גלגמש* (Warsaw: Stybel, 1924) – Hebrew verse translation

- **Translator:** Shaul Tchernichovsky (שאול טשרניחובסקי, 1875–1943). The translation was made **from German**, not from Akkadian. Ben-Yehuda labels it "תרגום: שאול טשרניחובסקי (מגרמנית)". Simchoni's introduction names Jensen and Ungnad (1911) as the available translations, and says G. E. Burckhardt's German retelling "became the model for all new translators". Hebrew Wikipedia (citing Sh. Shifra 2004) also says "from German".
- **Original edition:** Warsaw: A. J. Stybel, תרפ"ד (1924), with an introduction by Jacob Naftali Simchoni (יעקב נפתלי שמחוני). Reprinted in כתבי שאול טשרניחובסקי, vol. 6 (שירות ושירים), Tel Aviv: Am Oved, 1990 (Ben-Yehuda gives תש"ן; Hebrew Wikipedia gives 1995).

## Line numbering
There are **no line numbers** in the source. Tablet XI has 354 verse lines in Tchernichovsky's version (vs. 328 Akkadian lines in George), because his German source paraphrases and splits lines. Align by content.

## Assessment
**A literary classic, not a philological translation.** It is a rich biblical-style Hebrew poem ("וַיִּפְתַּח אֵאָה אֶת־פִּיהוּ", "סֻכַּת־קָנֶה! … כֹּתֶל!", "וָאֲשַׁלַּח אֶת־הָעוֹרֵב") that deliberately echoes Genesis 6–9. It is valuable for Hebrew register and diction, and as the canonical earlier Hebrew rendering readers know. It is **unreliable on content**:
- It is second-hand (from German) and based on 1900–1916 readings, with paraphrastic additions.
- Old name forms (Ninib, Nisir, Puzur-Amurri).
- At George XI 199 ff. it has **Ea** (not Enlil) board the ship and bless the couple.
- Atra-hasis is rendered "רַב־הַחָכְמָה" (a literal etymology of the name).
- Gaps are filled silently.
Do not use it as evidence for what the Akkadian says.
