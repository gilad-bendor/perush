### The text on its own terms
- **The sun's double course** (2, 26, 31–33, 42, 44): by day above, by night below the earth, through the sea and the *apsû* and the land of the dead, back to the east at dawn. Because he crosses everything, he sees everything; "above and below" is the hymn's formula for the whole world.
- **The netherworld is lit** (31, 37–38, 42, 57, 80): Šamaš "takes charge" of its gods (the ancient commentaries quote 31 to explain *Kūbu* and *malku* = Nergal / the Anunnaki), his rays go down into the *apsû*, the *laḫmu* of the sea see his light, and from Kurnugê he can bring a man back.
- **A working god** (41, 44–45): the sun "has no sleep", his face "grieves" at sunset but is never darkened (an eclipse is the exception the hymn denies), and no other god toils like him – praise for labour, not uniqueness.
- **The light as a net** (5, 83–94): the rays spread over the earth "like a *šuškallu*" (5) are the same net in which the criminal is caught (83). The net is the oath's punishment (85; the Stele of the Vultures' "great net" of the oath gods; Etana's serpent).
- **Šamaš as the god of divination** (21, 53–57, 76): the diviner (*bārû*) with his oil bowl and cedar, the dream-asker (*šāʾilu*) with his roasting plate, all kneel to him; the same verb *barû* "inspect" is used of Šamaš's look at the earth (21). Two manuscripts write *šāʾili* as a rebus "of the gods".
- **The judge** (58–64, 97–99): he "reveals the case" (*dīna šūpû*), lifts the litigant out of Ḫubur – the river of the dead and the river ordeal (62) – and gives sleep to the true man (60); he "does not carry the face"; the crooked judge sees prison, the bribe-taker "bears his guilt", the judge who acts as a father to the weak gets life.
- **Who needed Šamaš** (65–82): travellers on unknown roads, seafarers, merchants carrying their purse (with their weights in a leather bag), fugitives, prisoners of war, captives in jail, the man whose personal god is angry (75; Ludlul's theme), the sick man whose "fixed term" of illness has passed (77: the medical *adannu*), and the "dead" – a list built like Ps 107.
- **Social death in court** (92–93): father and brothers do not stand by the guilty man at his trial: the family's support, the normal condition of a Babylonian litigant, is withdrawn.
- **The adulterer under the sun** (88, 94): the sin "begins in the eyes" (*nīš īnī*), against the *tappû*, the partner; it is caught in a bronze bird-trap "and he did not know" – the god who sees in the dark exposes what was done at night.
- **Death before the fated day** (89): *šīmtu* fixes each man's day; the wicked are taken by a death that "provokes" them before it (as in Ashurbanipal's inscriptions).

### The text on its own terms
- **The ethics of trade** (101–121): the cheat with two weights in his bag, the lender who lends by the small measure and collects by the large, the usurer – all lose: the profit "is cut short", the purse is lost, "the curse of the people" overtakes him before his day, his heir does not inherit, his brothers do not enter his house. The honest weigher and the generous lender get the refrain: "it is good to Šamaš, he adds life", a family, wealth, an everlasting seed. Interest itself is lawful; the sin is fraud.
- **Who prays to Šamaš** (130–148): the whole of society that lives outside the city's protection – the poor man "with the hole of his mouth", the shepherd among enemies, the caravan in the land of terror, the merchant, the fisherman, the hunter, the fowler – and also the thief, the robber, the wandering dead and the lost ghost. The god of justice hears them all; 148 (`למעני, שַׁמַשׁ – אל תשנא אותם!`) is the only first person in the hymn.
- **The gestures of prayer** (131): prostration, kneeling, whispering and *labān appi*, the rubbing of the nose – a Babylonian gesture of submission absent from the Bible.
- **Divination** (149–155): Šamaš opens the ears of the diviners, "straightens" the omens, "sits in the sacrifices", cuts the decision for the four winds; his glance fills a hand, the diviner's bowl, more than heaven and earth.
- **The gods eat and drink** (156–161): on the twentieth of the month, Šamaš's feast day, he "rejoices", eats and drinks the beer of the harbour innkeeper and accepts the women's flour offerings – the feast as a meal of the god.
- **The strangers** (167–175): the people of "foolish tongue", the mountaineers, the *laḫmu* of the sea, the locust of the sea that goes down to the *apsû* – none is outside his light (the rhetorical questions of 174–175).
- **The mechanics of heaven** (182–183): the sun opens the gate of heaven each morning, lifting its bolt, peg and key – a lock of the ancient type, lifted from below (Judg 3:25).
- **Aya's "Rest!"** (200): the hymn ends at evening, in the bedroom of Ebabbar, where the bride Aya tells the tireless god to rest – Ps 19's bridegroom at the other end of the day.
