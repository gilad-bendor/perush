# Project 2: The Babylonian Theodicy

Wisdom corpus, project 2 (`../corpus.md`). Read `../../translation-guidelines.md`, `../corpus.md`, `../renderings.md`, `../translator-brief.md`, then this file and `../1-לודלול/project.md` (the Ludlul decisions bind wherever the two poems share a word).

## Scope
The whole poem as eBL edits it: 27 stanzas × 11 lines, **288 labels** (1–297; `109-110` and `111-119` are range labels for lost lines; stanzas IX–XI and XIV–XVII are badly broken). Two annotated files, drafted by sequential translators:
- `annotated-Th1.rtl.md`: **1–143** (stanzas I–XIII);
- `annotated-Th2.rtl.md`: **144–297** (stanzas XIV–XXVII).
Odd stanzas are the sufferer's, even stanzas the friend's (check each against the content; eBL gives no speaker labels). Stanza headings per `../renderings.md` §7: `## בית <N> (<lines>) – <speaker> – <syllable>`.

## Base edition
- **Rank 1:** eBL `L/2/1`, *Theodicy*, ed. A. C. Heinrich and T. D. N. Mitto, rev. E. Jiménez: `../../sources/eBL/Theodicy-SB/reading-edition.md`. Includes the newly recovered ancient title, new fragments and **the ancient commentary (`BabaLBCom1` = BM 66882+ from Borsippa, the same tablet as CCP 1.4; identified by translator 5)**, whose glosses (`X : Y`) stand in the ATF under each line: use them in the notes (the ancient scholars' reading).
- **Rank 3:** eBL's translation (Foster, Khatabe, Zerkly). Lambert 1960 and Oshima 2013/2014 are not available (`../../sources/_records-only--Wisdom-and-righteous-sufferer/`). The same commentary with Jiménez's English: `../../sources/CCP/1.4--P404917--Theodicy-commentary/` (2017).
- **Line numbering:** eBL's labels (= Lambert's 1–297 for the stanza grid).

## Why this text (guidelines §1, both purposes)
- **Bible:** Job's dialogue form (the friend's answers: the wicked rich fall, the gods' plan is far "like the midst of heaven", piety pays; the sufferer's counter-examples), Job 21 (the wicked prosper), 12:6, Qoh 9:11 (not to the swift the race), 7:15; Ps 37, 49, 73 (the prosperity of the wicked); Prov 1:7 (the fear of the god); Isa 55:8–9; the younger son preferred (Gen 25, 48); Gen 3 and the human condition (276–280: the gods gave mankind "twisted speech … lies and untruth"); Jer 17:9.
- **The ancient mind:** a dialogue of two learned men, politely abusive; the acrostic as a signature and a prayer; the rare vocabulary (the scribes' pleasure in synonyms, the ancient commentaries explaining them); justice, poverty and wealth in Babylonian society (the *šarû* and the *lapnu*); the gods' "far" will; the end: the friend concedes that the gods made men liars, and the sufferer prays to the god who threw him off, the goddess, and the king, "the shepherd, the sun of the people" (297).

## Open decisions for the user
Each section below lists its own; the main ones:
1. *kīna* (78): `נאמנה` ("truly", eBL) or Lambert's "O righteous one" (never `צדיק`, reserved).
2. Line 1: eBL's restoration *aḫī* "my brother" (followed) or the commentary's traces read *a-šiš?*.
3. *ṣurru* = `טוּחוֹת` (Job 38:36, Ps 51:8) or a plainer word ("heart, mind"); *pašallu* = `סְגוֹר` (`כֶּתֶם` trips the `תם` check).
4. *šagāšu* / *šaggāšu* = `רצח` / `רוצח` (an echo of `לֹא תִרְצָח`); *maṣû* `המספיק` (5) vs `נערך` (178, 261).
5. Line 297: "the shepherd, sun of the people" = the king (eBL; Ludlul I 55), not Šamaš.

### The text on its own terms
- **A disputation with manners** (every stanza): each speaker opens with lavish praise of the other – "my brother, the sagacious" (1), "careful, my friend" (12), "a spring is your heart" (23), "date palm, tree of wealth, my precious brother" (56), "your mind is a north wind" (67), "truly, acquirer of understanding" (78) – and turns at once to rebuke or refutation ("you have made your mind like an incompetent's", 14; "your sagacity you made babble", 35; "you have cast off truth", 79). Insult wrapped in praise is the form of the genre; the friend's "spring without bottom" (23) and the sufferer's "honeyed lips" (46) are compliments with a sting.
- **The orphan and the youngest son** (8–11): the sufferer's first grievance is family: the youngest child, the father "carried off by fate", the mother "returned to the land of no return", "no one to raise me". The friend's answer (16–17) is the consolation of the universal: "our fathers go the way of death, they cross the river Ḫubur, it was said of old" – death as an ancient decree and a river crossing.
- **Personal religion as patronage** (19–22, 70–75): wealth is always someone's gift ("someone made him rich", 19); "who looks at the face of the god acquires a protective goddess (*lamassu*); the throbbing one who reveres the goddess heaps up plenty" (21–22). The god and the goddess are a pair throughout (21–22, 70–71, 72–73, 80–81), and the sufferer answers in the friend's own two-line pattern, inverted.
- **Piety as forced labour** (72–75): in his youth the sufferer "sought the god's will", stroked his nose in prayer, "pulled the yoke" – *ilku*, the state corvée – "with no profit"; the Babylonian idea that men were made to bear the gods' labour, seen from below.
- **The animal exempla** (48–53, 59–62): the onager sated with grass and the lion that ate the choicest flesh never brought flour to a goddess; the friend answers that the onager meets the hunter's arrow and the lion the pit – "for the sin the lion committed". Animals are moral agents; the royal hunt (the onager and the lion are the king's quarry) is the instrument of justice.
- **The rich man and the king** (52–53, 63–64): the "lord of the face", whose wealth "they" increase, never weighed out gold to Mami; the friend: "like Girra, on a day not his fate, a prince will burn him" – justice through confiscation and the royal fire, a courtier's view of the world.
- **The gods' plan is far** (58, 82–87): "you are firm as the earth, yet the god's counsel is far"; "like the midst of heaven, the god's schemes are far"; men have "learnt" only the rites (84) – the plan itself is a drawing (*uṣurtu*, 79) no one can read. The distinction between the hidden plan and the learned cult is the Theodicy's theology.
- **The world upside down** (76–77): the cripple rises above, the fool goes ahead, the rogue is exalted, "and I have gone down" – the wisdom tradition's image of social chaos (Ludlul I 89–92).
- **Opting out of society** (133–143): the sufferer's last stanza here is a programme of anti-social acts, one per line: leave the house, desire no property, scorn the god's ordinances, trample the rites, slaughter a calf for himself, take to the open, break the dike and let the canal loose (the gravest crime in an irrigation society), roam "like one hiding", break into houses to drive off hunger, prowl the streets. It is the exact inverse of a citizen's duties.
- **The scribes' delight in words** (passim): three words for "where?" in three lines (5–7), four for "rites" in two (80–81), archaic pronouns (*kâša*, *kuāšu*, *ana* "I"), rare words for mother (*agarinnu*), help (*taḫanātu*), strength (*kubukku*); the acrostic forces assimilations (*ak-kâta*, *il-labān*) and a triple pun on *bēru* (calf, dike, league: 136–139).
- **The ancient readers** (the commentary BM 66882+): a Late Babylonian scholar glossed the poem word by word – "X means Y", "from the verb …", Sumerian sign equations, a notarikon of *agarinnu* ("a = son, rin = gather" → mother) and of Mami ("ma = create, me = people"), a quotation of *Malku* for *taḫanātu* – and, five times, "the wise man means the scribe". He sometimes reads against the text (*salittu* "estranged" as *salimtu* "reconciled", 40; *sagâ* as *sakkû* "rites", 84). The colophon calls the poem by its incipit, "[My brother], the sagacious", and calls itself "lemmata, oral explanations and (material for) a questioning by a master-scholar" – a school text.
- **The author** (the acrostic): Saggil-kīnam-ubbib, *mašmaššu* "who blesses god and king" – a court exorcist; his signature turns the dispute about divine justice into the work of a man whose craft was to reconcile angry gods (cf. 39–44: "the angry god … at prayer", "the estranged goddess returns at supplication").

### Lessons for the guidelines
- **An eBL "commentary" siglum may be an old, already-edited commentary**: `BabaLBCom1` = CCP 1.4 (BM 66882+). Check the museum number against CCP before treating it as new; use CCP's English translation of the glosses (eBL gives only the Akkadian).
- **Ancient commentaries decide renderings and expose eBL's lemma slips**: here they fixed *gana* = "come", *nakdu* = "reverent", *sagâ* = "rites", *kuppu* = "spring", and confirmed *itâr* against an emendation; their lemmata also count as manuscripts for brackets.
- **A first draft must be plene and unvocalized from the first line** – again: a fully vocalized draft had to be rewritten here (and vocalized defective forms like `הַמֻּשְׁלָם` trip the reserved-string check, `שלם`). Keep the rule at the top of every brief.

### Open decisions for the user
1. *ṣurru* = `טוּחוֹת` (Job 38:36, Ps 51:8), a rare word for a rare word – or a plainer `קֶרֶב` / `לֵבָב` (both collide: `קרב` = *qerbu*, `לבב` would blur *libbu*).
2. 176 *sarmāʾu* rendered `*מוֹטָה?*`, the word of *nīru* (240), because the ancient commentary glosses *sarmāʾu* = *nīru*; alternatives: `…` (untranslated) or a transliteration.
3. *maṣû* "correspond, equal" = `נערך` (178, 261) beside Th1 5 `המספיק` ("suffice"): one lemma, two senses – or unify.
4. 235 *ša ḫarḫari*: eBL's lemma "chain" (`(איש) השלשלת`) or *ḫarḫaru* "rogue" (`הנוכל`, as 221).
5. *pašallu* `סְגוֹר`: `כֶּתֶם` (Job 28:16, 19) would be the more usual word, but its plene form contains the reserved string `תם`; the coordinator may allow-list *pašallu* for `תם` in `wisdom_tools.py` and restore `כֶּתֶם`.
6. *šagāšu* / *šaggāšu* = `רצח` / `רוצח` (267, 238): the natural word for murder, at the cost of the Decalogue's `לֹא תִרְצָח` – or `קטל` (but that is *nērtu*, 284) / `שחט`.
7. *redû* "drive (an animal)" `נוהג` (248) also renders *mû* "custom" (`נוהג`, Ludlul II 17, 29) – different parts of speech; or `מוליך`.
8. 297: the "shepherd, sun of the people" is the king (eBL, with Ludlul I 55), not Šamaš (the coordinator's brief); the last word follows eBL's new commentary fragment F.78 (*lītânni* "may he find (choose) me"); CCP read the commentary there as *lilabbir* "may he make (my life) long".
9. 233 *īteme*: 3rd person (eBL, and this is the friend's stanza) or 1st person (CCP: "I have become deaf", = Ludlul I 71).
10. 259 is obscure (`*לא־מגוזם*`, after eBL's lemma); eBL's translation follows Lambert's very different sense.
11. *ašû* `בעלי־החיים` (162): avoids `חַיָּה` (Gilgameš *umāmu*) and `יצורים` (יצר, Gen 2:7).
12. *nibītu* `כינוי` (217, 263): `קרא` is reserved for *nabû*, but *nibītu* is *nabû*'s own noun; `קְרִיאָה` would show the root (and the echo of naming).

### The text on its own terms
- **The concession** (276–286): after twenty-five stanzas the friend grants the sufferer's case and explains it theologically: the three creator gods – the king Narru (Enlil), the sage Zulummar (Ea) who pinched off the clay, the mother Mami who shaped it – gave mankind "twisted speech, lies and untruths, permanently". Injustice is not a failure of the gods' justice but a property of the creature they made; the same verb *šarāku* "bestow" (279–280) then describes how men "bestow" slander on the poor (284). The friend's concession ends the dispute without restoring anything.
- **The reversal of the social order** (181–187, 245–253, 267–275): king's son and pauper's son, the malt-keeper and the gold-measurer, the eater of greens and the rich man's son eating carob; the father hauling the boat while the firstborn sprawls in bed, the heir roaming while the "second" son feeds the poor; seven D-stem verbs of what "they" do – exalt the murderer, abase the lowly, foster the wicked, drive off the loyal, fill the criminal's treasury, empty the wretched man's granary, strengthen the tyrant. Babylonian society is seen from the scribal middle: the officials (malt-keeper, measurer), the magnate (*bēl pāni*), the *muškēnu*.
- **The friend's theodicy of decree** (196–198, 254–264): long life is a god's gift, wealth and poverty are "decreed from of old" (*šiāmu*, the verb of fate); the divine heart is "far as the midst of heaven" (256 = 82); even nature shows that the gods' order is not ours – Aruru's creatures are born the weaker first, the stronger second (258–263).
- **The scribe's education and fall** (199–209): the sufferer learned from a "wise one" whose tablet was "full of the stylus", who opened to him the "knotted and locked" things – the scribal self-image of hidden knowledge; now he prowls the streets "for the hole of his mouth". The acrostic's author is such a scholar.
- **The ancient commentator's scribes** (201, 202, 206, 217, 223): five times in this half the Late Babylonian commentary says that a wise man of the poem – "the knowing one", "the fowler", "the wise", "the broad-minded", even "the one who amasses" – "means the scribe" (once: the god Ea). The scholars read the Theodicy as a poem about themselves.
- **The commentator at work** (passim): notarikon of rare words and names (*šalbābu* "wise" = *šal* "wide" + *ba* "to do"; *Sulummar* = "who carries splendour"; *kikurrû*), lexical lists (*išpiku* = *rugbu*), alternative explanations ("alternatively …"), a whole proverb quoted (218), the equation *nadû* = *ezēbu* (295), and the recurrent gloss *lā kittu* "untruth" for sin, slander and twisted speech (273, 279, 284): for him, evil is first of all falsehood.
- **Humility, the yoke and its reward** (166, 215–220, 240): the friend's ethic is submission – be "humble, submissive", "keep low", "follow the way of the god", "drag the god's yoke" – with a concrete promise: the corvée basket removed, a head lifted, bread that is meagre but regular. Behind it is the Babylonian idea that men were created to bear the gods' toil (Atra-ḫasīs).
- **The world read as an omen** (180, 243): the sufferer "inspects" (*barû*, the diviner's verb) the people and the world as a diviner inspects a liver, and finds "the signs contradictory"; 214's *šummu*, the "if"-clause of laws and omens, is the form of all Babylonian knowledge.
- **The demon the god does not block** (244): suffering comes from demons such as the *šarrabu*, and the god simply does not stop them – a different theology from Ludlul's Marduk who strikes and heals.
- **The firstborn and the second** (245–250, 260–263): in law and custom the firstborn inherits; the poem twice inverts it – socially (the idle heir) and naturally (the weak first calf) – the same tension the Bible resolves by divine choice.
- **Social death in the city** (209, 222–224, 252–253, 283–286): the street, the square, the drinking company with its beer jars, the poor man maligned "like a thief", "snuffed out like an ember" because "he has no treasury": poverty itself is the crime.
- **The final prayer and the personal gods** (287–297): the sufferer asks mercy first of his friend (with a divine epithet, *rēmēnû*), describes the modesty of his life (quiet in the square, voice low, head down, no flattery), and prays to the god who "threw him off" and the goddess who "ceased" from him (= Ludlul I 43–44) – and to the king, "the shepherd, the sun of the people", to "find him as a god would". The exorcist "who blesses god and king" ends with god, goddess and king.
- **Wordplay and form** (passim): the forced acrostic spellings *aš-ša*, *aš-šī*, *aš-šu* (XVI); stanza XXV with every line in *u-* and seven D-stem plurals; stanza XXVI with every line in *šar/sar*, punning *šarru* "king", *šarratu* "queen", *šarû* "rich", *šarāku* "give", *sarrātu* "lies", *šarrāqiš* "like a thief"; *lēʾû* "competent" of the sufferer (254) against the god's *lēʾûtu* (257) and the "not-competent" crushed (274); *šibqū* "schemes" of the god (82) and of the sufferer (265).

### Lessons for the guidelines
- **eBL's round brackets in N are not ours**: *( … )* there marks words found in only some manuscripts; copying them into the `>` line as `(…)` would read as "added for sense". Never translate them (Th1's rule), and never echo the parentheses.
- **A reserved string can hide inside an ordinary word's plene spelling** (`כֶּתֶם` → `כתם` ⊃ `תם`): grep every new word against the RESERVED list as a string before choosing it.
- **eBL's lemma, its translation and the ancient commentary disagree more often in broken stanzas** (147, 162, 230, 235, 259 here): parse the form, follow the lemma or the commentary, and say which in the note; the commentary's quotations (even of a root, 207) count as a witness for brackets, and a newly identified commentary fragment (F.78, 297) can restore a word.
- **A brief's identification can be wrong in a key line**: the "shepherd, sun of the people" of 297 is the king (eBL, Ludlul I 55), not Šamaš – keep briefs to "look for".
