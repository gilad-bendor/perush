## Why this text (guidelines §1, both purposes)
- **Bible:** royal hubris against a god's house and its punishment (Isa 14:4–21; Ezek 28; Dan 4–5; Gen 11:1–9); the king's dream and the omen (Gen 41; Dan 2, 4); the barbarian enemy summoned by the god from the mountains (Isa 5:26–30, 10:5–6; Jer 5:15–17; Hab 1:6–11; Deut 28:49–50); famine and its prices (2 Kgs 6:25, 7:1; Lam 4–5); the curses of Deut 28 and Lev 26 (what is cursed: produce, cattle, the house, the family); the city returned to the wild (Isa 13:19–22, 34:11–15; Zeph 2:13–15; Jer 50:39–40); the curse fulfilled "so it was" (cf. `וַיְהִי כֵן`; Josh 6:26 / 1 Kgs 16:34).
- **The ancient mind:** how a dynasty's rise and fall were explained (a god gives and takes kingship; the goddess abandons her city); what a king may and may not do to a temple (building needs the god's consent, asked by extispicy); the gods as a family who "cool Enlil's heart"; the Gutians as the image of the non-human enemy; Sumerian historiography as theology (Naram-Suen did not in fact destroy the Ekur).

### Key findings (run 4)
**Bible.**
1. **The sin is not pride but changing the god's word (99).** In 148 lines there is no word of pride, no claim to divinity (though the historical Naram-Suen wrote his name with the god-sign), no ascent "to heaven" by the king: the wall "reaches heaven" (42) as Inana's gift. The king asks the omen twice, the kid has "no building" in it (95, 97), and he "changed Enlil's word" (99: the very words of LU 167 "An – his word cannot be changed"). So the closest biblical structure is not Isa 14 / Ezek 28 / Gen 11 (hubris), but **David's temple denied** (2 Sam 7; 1 Chr 17:4; 22:8 – a warrior-king asks to build a house and is refused) combined with **Saul unanswered** (1 Sam 28:6 – "neither by dreams nor by Urim", then the forbidden act). David accepts; Naram-Suen builds by force – and his "building" is the demolition.
2. **Daniel 4 in five places**: the kingdom given "to whom he will" (6 ↔ Dan 4:14, 22, 29 `יִתְּנִנַּהּ`); the city "reaching heaven" (42, 72; *us₂* ↔ Aram. `מטא`, Dan 4:8, 19); the royal insignia taken (67–68 ↔ Dan 4:28, 5:20); the night vision (86 ↔ Dan 2:19, 4:2); **seven years** (92 ↔ `וְשִׁבְעָה עִדָּנִין`, Dan 4:13, 22, 29); and the temple cut "at its roots … at its branches" (115–117 ↔ `גֹּדּוּ אִילָנָא וְקַצִּצוּ עַנְפוֹהִי … עִקַּר שָׁרְשׁוֹהִי`, Dan 4:11–12) – ETCSL's "top" for *pa* hides the tree.
3. **"Its weight they carry off like (spoil in) battle" (82):** *nam-dugud* = Akk. *kabtūtu*, the root of `כָּבוֹד` (*kbt*): the city's "honour/weight" carried away ↔ `גָּלָה כָבוֹד מִיִּשְׂרָאֵל כִּי נִלְקַח אֲרוֹן הָאֱלֹהִים` (1 Sam 4:21–22). With `כבוד` and `גלה` reserved, the Hebrew `כובד … נושאים` shows that the Sumerian has the image but not the exile verb.
4. **Looking at the holy (129–130):** the people see "the bed-chamber that knows no daylight", Akkad looks "into the gods' holy chest" ↔ Num 4:20 (`לִרְאוֹת כְּבַלַּע אֶת הַקֹּדֶשׁ`), 1 Kgs 8:12 (`לִשְׁכֹּן בָּעֲרָפֶל`), 1 Sam 6:19 (Beth-shemesh looked into the ark). Rendered `ארגז` (1 Sam 6:8's box), not `ארון`, so that the motif-parallel is not manufactured at word level.
5. **"Eating a taboo" (132)** = Deut 14:3 `לֹא תֹאכַל כָּל תּוֹעֵבָה`, word for word (*an-zil* = *ikkibu*): the *laḫama* figures are burned "though they had eaten no abomination" – and the real "eater of abomination" is the king.
6. **Isaiah 60 is the biblical picture of Agade's glory (19, 43–53):** foreigners like birds (Isa 60:8), gates never shut (60:11), wealth by ship (60:9), camels/asses loaded with tribute (60:6, Neh 13:15), the nations' kings (Ps 72:10–11), the precious woods of the sanctuary (134 ↔ 60:13). The Bible's eschatological Zion has the shape of a Sumerian imperial capital.
7. **Lev 26:19 in 121–122, in order:** the rain goes up instead of down (ETCSL's "went back" is not *gi₄*: no `שוב`), and "the pride (*teš₂*) of the land was changed" ↔ `וְשָׁבַרְתִּי אֶת גְּאוֹן עֻזְּכֶם וְנָתַתִּי אֶת שְׁמֵיכֶם כַּבַּרְזֶל`; Ezek 24:21 calls the sanctuary itself `גְּאוֹן עֻזְּכֶם`. 121 is the reverse of Isa 55:10 (rain that does not return).
8. **Psalm 74** (axes in the sanctuary, fire) for 108–117, 133 – with the decisive [divergence]: there the enemy, here the land's own king. And Deut 7:25 / 2 Kgs 19:18: burning images is commanded in Israel, the crime here.
9. **The happy city by generations (29–33)** has the order of Zech 8:4–5 (old men and women, then children playing) and Jer 31:12; the gifts of counsel and speech removed by the gods (70–71) ↔ Ezek 7:26, Isa 3:1–3, 29:14, Obad 8, Jer 49:7.
10. **Cognates via Akkadian:** *gen₆* = *kânu* `נכון` (83: the "firm" kingship of 2 Sam 7:16, 1 Kgs 2:46 – and Sargon's name *Šarru-kīn*); *eĝer* = *arkatu* `אחרית` (84); *silim* = *šalāmu* `שלום` (125–126, Ps 122:7); *giĝ₄* `שקל` (105, the thirty shekels of Zech 11:12–13); *ugu₄-bi* / *uqūpu* `קוף` (21, 1 Kgs 10:22); *le-um* / *lēʾu* `לוח` (20); *guru₇* `כרי`; *gu-za* `כסא`; *zag-mu* ~ `ראש השנה` (53, Ezek 40:1).
11. **Gen 10:8–10:** Kiš, Uruk and Agade (2–4) are the cities of Nimrod's kingdom (`בָּבֶל וְאֶרֶךְ וְאַכַּד`), and Meluḫḫa, "the black land" (48), became Kush in the 1st millennium – Nimrod is "son of Kush" (cautious proposal).

**The ancient mind.**
1. **History as theology:** the poem moves the fall of Akkad (two generations later, under Šar-kali-šarri) onto its most famous king, and turns his real restoration of the Ekur into a demolition. The logic is that of omen-consent: building without the god's "yes" is destroying.
2. **A city as a goddess's household:** Inana builds Agade like a youth his first house, a girl her women's quarters (8–11), cannot sleep (24), fills the granaries, seals the silos with clay (28), gives each age-group its gift (29–33) – and leaves "like one abandoning the girl of her chamber" (61). The ruin is the break-up of a household.
3. **Gifts that can be taken back:** kingship (6), the insignia "given" (68), counsel, speech, wisdom, radiance, mooring, weapons (66–76): each god takes back exactly what is his. The *me*-like word of the Ekur (57) needs no reason.
4. **Akkad's world economy:** Meluḫḫa (Indus: elephants, monkeys, water buffalo, ebony, ivory), Marḫaši (eastern Iran), Elam, Subir, the Amorites; tribute rolls on writing boards (20); copper, tin, lapis; Sumer's goods towed upstream (45); the quay and its merchants (37). The poem knows the first empire as a trade network.
5. **Divination and dreams:** the night vision (*maš₂-ĝi₆*) and the kid of extispicy (*maš₂*) share one sign; the king tells no one (87); the omen is asked twice (94–97); the gods answer by silence.
6. **The temple as body, tree and granary:** it bows "like a dead young man" (118), is cut "at its roots … at its branches" (115–117), its gates bear names that are blessings ("from which grain is never cut", "of well-being", 123–125) and the deed inverts each name; its inner room "knows no daylight" (129); its *laḫama* guardians are innocent (132).
7. **The structure of reversal:** every element of the building (1–56) returns dismantled (57–148), and the curses of part 2 will reverse them again (the sleepless city, the acquaintances, the young women in the women's quarters, the quay, the grain).

### Key findings (run 5)
**Bible.**
1. **Uncreation, by three fixed renderings (231–236 with 171, 175, 241).** The clay "returns" (*gi₄* = `שוב`) to the Abzu, the grain to the furrow, the timber to the forest, each "cursed" (*nam ku₅* = `ארר`) by the god who gave it; the priestesses "make the child return" (241, the same *gi₄*). Before that the land had gone back to "the day when a city was fashioned" (171; ETCSL's "before" is not in the Sumerian: no `טרם`), and "the clouds did not rain, the *mašgurum* did not grow" (175: *šeĝ₃* = `המטיר`, *mu₂* = `צמח`, Gen 2:5's two verbs, negated). Gen 3:17–19 (`אֲרוּרָה הָאֲדָמָה … עַד שׁוּבְךָ אֶל הָאֲדָמָה … וְאֶל עָפָר תָּשׁוּב`) and Gen 2:5 meet here in one poem – but for a city, and for a king's disobedience (99), not for man's. This is the strongest pair of the lament corpus with the creation texts.
2. **2 Kings 6–7 in the famine (176–180):** the same price formula (commodity, measure, "for a shekel") with cognate units (`שקל`, `סאה`, `מנה`; `קב` cautiously), and the prices as the sign that the word was fulfilled (*ur₅* "thus" in 180 and 272 ↔ `כִּדְבַר יְהוָה`, 2 Kgs 7:16).
3. **Exact word pairs that only fixed renderings make visible:** `גדע` + `זרוע` (245 ↔ 1 Sam 2:31, the curse on Eli's house for profaning the holy; Jer 48:25); `שׂם עין` on a city for evil (223 ↔ Amos 9:4, Jer 21:10; *ĝar* = `שׂם`); `שׂימו … צברים` of heads (189 ↔ 2 Kgs 10:8); the cistern "filled" with the dead (214 ↔ Jer 41:9); `קלל` and `ארר` side by side (224, 232 ↔ Gen 12:3).
4. **Lamentations 4–5 and the curse list:** those who ate delicacies lie in the streets (249 ↔ Lam 4:5), the devalued gold (242–244 ↔ Lam 4:1–2; Isa 60:17 is the exact inverse as blessing), mothers and families in famine (237–238 ↔ Lam 4:10), foxes on the holy mount (257 ↔ Lam 5:18), the wall mourning (227 ↔ Lam 2:8, 18), the roads mourning (265 "grass of lament" ↔ Lam 1:4), the gates sunk in the mud (168 ↔ Lam 2:9).
5. **The city returned to the wild (255–269) reads like Isaiah 13 and 34 and Zephaniah 2:** demons howling (Isa 34:14), the grief-bird nesting in the gate (Isa 34:15, Zeph 2:14 "in the threshold"), thorns and grass (Isa 34:13), no passer-by for the beasts (Ezek 14:15), salt (Judg 9:45, Deut 29:22, Ps 107:34), and Jer 17:6 `אֶרֶץ מְלֵחָה וְלֹא תֵשֵׁב` bridges 269 and 270 exactly. The curse on resettlement (270–271) = Josh 6:26, and its fulfilment "so it was" (272–280) = 1 Kgs 16:34 `כִּדְבַר יְהוָה`.
6. **The god who fasts (209):** Enlil enters his bedchamber and lies down fasting, like David (2 Sam 12:16), Darius (Dan 6:19) or Ahab (1 Kgs 21:4) – a human rite performed by the god; the gods then "cool his heart with cool water" (211; Prov 25:25, 15:1; Moses in Exod 32:12) – but to obtain a curse, not mercy.
7. **Mourning rites (193–208):** seven days and seven nights (Job 2:13, the same phrase); tearing the hair and (perhaps) cutting with the blade (205–206: the pair Deut 14:1 forbids); drums as instruments of lament (a divergence: in the Bible the drum is joy); a priestly lament singer, *gala* (absent in the Bible; Jer 9:16 has keening women); the ancestors weeping (207–208 ↔ Rachel, Jer 31:14). And the god's "small sanctuary" of reed (194 ↔ Ezek 11:16 `לְמִקְדָּשׁ מְעַט`; our proposal).
8. **The Gutians (149–175):** the god's flood (*a-ma-ru*) and instrument (Isa 8:7, 10:5; Ezek 38:4 `וְהוֹצֵאתִי`), "not counted in the Land" (154 ↔ Deut 32:21 `לֹא עָם`, and inversely Num 23:9 `וּבַגּוֹיִם לֹא יִתְחַשָּׁב`), the human–animal mixture (156 ↔ Dan 4:13), small birds rather than the eagle of Deut 28:49 (158), the net (159 ↔ Hos 7:12, Hab 1:15), the roads and rivers emptied (162–163 ↔ Judg 5:6, Isa 33:8, 21), "the Lord's flock" taken (164 ↔ Jer 13:17).
9. **Echo control in the other direction:** `קְדֵשָׁה` is the etymological cognate of *nu-gig* (*qadištu*, 241) but would import Deut 23:18's sexual sense: transliterated; `ארובות` (219) and `שבעת ימים ושבעת לילות` (199) are the natural Hebrew and real parallels: kept and tagged; Obad 15's `יֵעָשֶׂה` was *not* used for *du₃* "build" (212–213): `תיבנה`.
10. **The Ur III version (A–F)** lacks line 99 (the sin named as "changing Enlil's word"), the four similes of demolition (108–111) and line 190 (honest and false heads confounded); it has 115 after 127–128; it writes Naram-Suen without the divine determinative (B3); it ends segment F with the kingship formula of line 5 (F7). The OB version made the theology explicit and the destruction more poetic.

**The ancient mind.**
1. **The enemy as the non-human human:** the Gutians have "human intelligence", but are "not set among people", "not counted", "know no binding", have "the counsel of a dog and the face of a monkey" – a definition of barbarism by what is missing: place, count, bond.
2. **The economy of disaster:** messengers and boats stop, herds and herdsmen are driven off, prisoners guard, brigands sit on the roads, gardens grow inside the walls, prices rise six-hundredfold (barley ½ *sila* per shekel against about 300), the dead are unburied (and so become restless ghosts), dogs band like troops, gold sells as silver, the poor sell children, the strong man lies idle.
3. **Mourning as liturgy aimed at a god:** the drums "like Iškur", the cries "alas my city / its people / the Ekur", the hair and the blade, seven days and nights – and it works: the god lies down fasting. A lament is a technique for reaching a god's heart.
4. **The divine family council:** eight gods, including Inana herself, "cool" Enlil's heart and pray – and the cooling is the curse: a god's anger is appeased by giving it its revenge (talio between cities, 212–213).
5. **Curses as the inverse of blessings:** almost every curse undoes a line of 1–56 (the old women's counsel, the young men's arm, the girls' play, the acquaintances' meals, the sleepless joyful city, the gold-filled granaries, the sealed silo, the quay); the curses of materials undo creation itself.
6. **Utu as witness:** "now, before Utu, on this day – so it was": the sun, who sees all and before whom oaths are sworn, witnesses that the curse was carried out; the narrator's "this day" is the hearer's present, Agade's ruin. And the last word is praise for Inana, the city's own goddess, who built, abandoned, cursed – and is praised.
